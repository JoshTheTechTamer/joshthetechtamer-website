How to spot them

The usual scams, and what they look like.

Most scams run the same few scripts. Once you’ve seen them, they’re a lot easier to catch.

Fake “virus” pop-ups

A full-screen warning, sometimes with an alarm sound, says your computer is infected and gives a number to call. Microsoft, Apple and Google don’t put phone numbers in warnings like that. Close the browser, or hold the power button if it won’t close.

Tech support calls

Someone says they’re from Microsoft, Apple, Amazon or your internet company and your computer has a problem. Real companies don’t call out of the blue about a virus. Hang up. If you’re unsure, call the company yourself at a number you look up.

Bank, Amazon and delivery texts

“Unusual charge on your account.” “Your package can’t be delivered.” “Your account is locked.” Don’t tap the link. Open the company’s own app, or call the number on the back of your card, and check from there.

Gift cards and odd payments

A surprise caller pushing you to pay by gift card, wire, cryptocurrency or a payment app is a big warning sign. Some real businesses take these, so check the request on your own. Never pay a surprise caller with gift card codes.

“Grandma, I’m in trouble” calls

“It’s me. I’m in trouble. Please don’t tell Mom.” Sometimes a “lawyer” or “officer” gets on the line. Voices can be faked now. Hang up and call your family member at the number you already have.

Romance and too-good-to-be-true

A new online friend who can never meet and soon has a money problem. A prize you have to pay to collect. An investment that “can’t lose.” Sending money to someone you’ve never met, or paying to win, is your cue to stop.

Four red flags. Pressure (act right now). Secrecy (don’t tell anyone, stay on the line). Strange payment (gift cards, wire, crypto). Access (codes, passwords, control of your computer). Any one of them is reason enough to stop and check with someone you trust.

Is it hacked?

Signs someone got into your account.

  • Friends say they got strange messages or friend requests from you.
  • Your password suddenly stops working.
  • You get emails or texts about sign-ins, password changes or new devices that weren’t you.
  • There are sent emails, posts or purchases you didn’t make.
  • Your recovery phone number or backup email changed.
  • Sign-in codes show up that you didn’t ask for.

Seeing one of these?

Start with your email, on a phone or computer you trust. Change the password, then turn on 2-step verification. The checklist at the top has the order.

If a sign-in code shows up that you didn’t ask for, don’t share it with anyone. A surprise code can come from a sign-in attempt, a password reset, or someone typing the wrong details. It doesn’t prove they have your password. Open the official app or site yourself and look at the account activity.

Lock it down

Make the next scam harder to pull off.

Start with your email, then your bank, then everything else.

Password managers

Use a different password for every account, so one leak doesn’t open the rest. A password manager remembers them for you, and there’s one built into iPhones, Android phones and Chrome. A notebook kept safe at home beats using the same password everywhere.

2-step verification

A second check, like a code on your phone or a tap on a prompt, when someone signs in. A stolen password alone isn’t enough anymore. Turn it on for email first, and never read that code to anyone who calls or texts you.

Recovery info

Make sure each important account has a current recovery phone number and backup email. That’s how you get back in if you’re locked out. Check them once a year, and any time you change phone numbers.

For the person everyone calls

Protecting older family members.

Scammers go after older adults a lot. A little planning together helps, and it works best without a lecture.

Talk about it, kindly

Share the common scripts above, like the fake pop-up and the “grandchild in trouble” call, so they’re easy to spot. Keep it friendly, like a chat over coffee.

Make a “call me first” rule

Before anyone sends money, buys gift cards or lets someone onto the computer, they call you (or me) first. A real request can wait five minutes.

Pick a family code word

A word only your family knows. If a caller says they’re a grandchild in trouble and can’t give it, hang up and call that grandchild directly.

Save the numbers that matter

Put the bank, family and a trusted helper in their phone contacts, so they aren’t searching for numbers under pressure.

Turn on call and spam filters

Most phones and carriers can silence unknown callers or flag likely spam. With their permission, set up 2-step verification and recovery info too.

No shame if it happens

If they get fooled, thank them for telling you. People who feel embarrassed stop asking for help, and scammers count on that.

Report it

Where to report a scam.

Reporting warns other people and creates a record. It doesn’t always get money back, so if money or a card was involved, call your bank first.

Your bank or card company

Call the number on the back of your card, not a number from a text, email or caller. Tell them what happened and ask what they can do.

Good questions

Scam help: quick answers.

A pop-up says my computer is infected and gives a phone number. Should I call?

No. Real companies like Microsoft and Apple don’t put phone numbers in warning pop-ups. Don’t call, don’t let anyone connect to your computer, and don’t pay. Close the browser, or turn the computer off if it won’t close, and text me.

I already let someone onto my computer. What now?

Don’t panic. Turn the computer off or unplug it from the internet. If you shared banking details or paid anything, call your bank using the number on the back of your card. Then call me, and we’ll check the computer and change the passwords that matter, from a safe device.

Can you get my hacked account back?

I’ll work through every recovery option the company offers with you, step by step. I can’t promise every account comes back, because the company makes that decision.

What does scam help cost?

Same as any tech help: $50 an hour. Texting me first is free. Visits at your place have a one-hour minimum. And I’ll never ask you for gift cards.

How do I keep this from happening again?

Turn on 2-step verification for your email and important accounts, use a different password for each one, and pause before you click. The lock-it-down and family tips on this page walk you through it.
